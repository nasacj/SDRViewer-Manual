BL618 Wi-Fi board

No computer to carry and no Raspberry Pi to set up: a development board costing a few dollars can be the rtl_tcp server. The RTL-SDR plugs into it, it opens its own Wi-Fi hotspot, and the phone joins that and receives. It runs from a power bank, works a few seconds after power-on, and goes in a pocket to the airport fence or up a hill.

The firmware and its full instructions are on GitHub: nasacj/rtlsdr-iphone-bridge. This page covers what to buy, how to wire it, how to set up the app and what the lights mean; for building and flashing the firmware, follow the steps there.

What you need

ItemNotes

The board's Type-C port already has a USB-serial chip behind it, used for flashing and for the log, so no separate serial adapter is needed.

Wiring

Lay the board front up, antenna at the top, Type-C at the bottom. Four wires: the bottom two pins of the left row (5V, GND) and the 5th and 6th from the bottom of the right row (USB_DP, USB_DM), to a USB-A socket; the RTL-SDR plugs into the socket.

The top pin of the right row is also GND (J2-1) and can be used instead of the one on the left.

Before and after wiring:

  • Unplug the Type-C cable while wiring, and plug it back in only when all four wires are on.
  • Do not swap 5V and GND: that can destroy the RTL-SDR. Check again before powering up: the bottom pin of the left row is GND, the one above it 5V. Trust your own cable over any colour code; if in doubt, check the socket with a multimeter — the two outer contacts are VBUS and GND, the two inner ones data.
  • Swapping USB_DM and USB_DP damages nothing; the RTL-SDR is simply not recognised. Swap them back.
  • The data pair carries 480 Mbit/s USB. Keep the two wires short and together.
  • The same names are printed on the back of the board, but seen from the back the two rows swap sides. Go by the front.

The RTL-SDR is powered from the board's 5V pin through a diode, so it gets a few tenths of a volt less than 5 V, which RTL-SDRs normally accept. If it is not recognised, or keeps disconnecting, suspect power first: a stronger charger, or measure the voltage on the 5V pin.

Flashing the firmware

The full steps for building and flashing are in the project's README. The main points:

  • Building needs an x86_64 Linux machine (physical, virtual or cloud); flashing works from Linux or a Mac.
  • Before every flash, put the board in download mode by hand: hold IO2 → press and release EN → release IO2. The two buttons are either side of the Type-C port, EN on the left, IO2 on the right.
  • Press EN after flashing and the new firmware runs.

First boot: the Wi-Fi name and password

At power-on the board opens its own hotspot, named SDR-Bridge- plus four hex digits, for example SDR-Bridge-3F2A. Every board has its own password: twelve characters drawn at random on first boot and kept on the board, the same on every boot after that and across reflashing.

The password is printed only on the serial log. Connect the board's Type-C to a computer, open the serial port at 2,000,000 baud, and press EN:

tio -b 2000000 /dev/ttyUSB0                  # Linux
tio -b 2000000 /dev/tty.usbserial-XXXX       # Mac (brew install tio first)

The boot log has a line like this:

[wifi] network "SDR-Bridge-3F2A" up, password xxxxxxxxxxxx

Note the name and password, and you will not need a computer again.

Why not a fixed password: rtl_tcp has no authentication at all, and anyone on the Wi-Fi can retune the receiver and read every sample, so the Wi-Fi password is the only lock. The board can also join your phone's Personal Hotspot instead, so the phone keeps its internet; see "Wi-Fi settings" in the project's README.

Setting up the app

The board's Wi-Fi carries about 30 Mbit/s, so three things differ from a computer: use UDP, use a sample rate of 1.024 MSPS, and send ADS-B as magnitudes only.

  1. Join the board's hotspot

    In the iPhone's Wi-Fi settings, choose SDR-Bridge-XXXX and enter the password. The hotspot has no internet; the iPhone keeps using mobile data for that, so other apps are unaffected.

  2. Add the receiver

    Open the receiver editor as in Connect in the app and fill it in like this:

    FieldEnter
    TCP / UDPUDP
    Hostrtlsdr.local, or 192.168.7.1 if that does not connect
    Port1235

    Port 1234 is standard TCP rtl_tcp and works too, but over Wi-Fi UDP is faster, and when data is lost the app knows exactly where. The first time it connects, iOS asks whether the app may access the local network: choose Allow.

  3. Set the sample rate to 1.024 MSPS

    In the Receiver page's settings, under Sample rate, choose 1.024 MSPS. It applies to the Receiver, Radio and ATC pages (stop playing before changing it).

    Why: a sample is two bytes, so 1.024 MSPS is 16.4 Mbit/s, which the Wi-Fi carries easily; 2.4 MSPS is 38.4 Mbit/s, more than the board's Wi-Fi can carry, and the losses sound like audio breaking up.

  4. ADS-B: turn on magnitudes only

    In the ADS-B page's settings, turn on UDP: magnitude only (half the data). The switch can be turned on only when the receiver uses UDP, and it is on by default.

    ADS-B receives at 2 MSPS, and full I/Q is 32 Mbit/s, at the edge of the board's Wi-Fi. Decoding ADS-B needs only the signal's strength, so sending magnitudes halves the data and nothing is lost.

Then press play. Scanning for FM stations on the Radio page is the easiest way to see that everything works.

What the lights mean

Away from a computer, on a power bank, the board's lights tell you what it is doing. There is an RGB light and two single-colour lights: a yellow one marked W and a white one marked C on the silkscreen. All three speak the same language:

slow blink = waiting, steady = ready, breathing = working, fast blink = trouble.

LightAnswersSlow blinkSteadyBreathingFast blink
Yellow (W)Is the RTL-SDR OK?no RTL-SDR plugged inRTL-SDR ready—plugged in but cannot be opened, or USB delivers nothing
White (C)How far have Wi-Fi and the phone got?Wi-Fi up, waiting for a phonephone connected, not playingstreaming—
RGBIs the stream healthy?—red: Wi-Fi not up yetblue: streaming, all wellred/blue: data lost on the USB side; purple/blue: Wi-Fi cannot keep up, data lost
  • White off: Wi-Fi is not up yet.
  • The RGB light is off when nothing is playing and nothing is wrong. When a stream ends by itself (the RTL-SDR pulled out, say) it shows red for 3 seconds.
  • A fault keeps its pattern for a few seconds afterwards, so a glance shows that data was just lost.
  • Walking around with the board, where it starts flashing purple and blue is where the Wi-Fi is not enough: move closer, or lower the sample rate.
